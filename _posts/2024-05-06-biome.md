We outline connections between the gut microbiome, autoimmune conditions, neuropathic pain, eye pain, chemical intolerance, and a specific set of "overactive" mental illnesses. All seem to be connected to a sensory processing disorder. This is joint work with Luca Estinto.

**The question:** does one shared underlying state explain why these conditions turn up together in the same people, or do several separate ones?

We propose there is collection of disorders characterized by sensory processing issues. This collection of symptoms affects many and is rarely viewed holistically. We propose to view them holistically and approach their treatment as such -- keeping in mind that autoimmune disorders usually are best detected genetically, and medication-wise sensory processing is best treated using tricylic antidepressants, GABA analogues, and glasses prescribed by a behavioral optometrist.

> ### ⚠ What this is and isn't
>
> This essay generates hypotheses. It has not tested them. Most of the mechanism below comes from mice, the human evidence is correlational, and no study so far measures all of these conditions in the same people. The last section says what would count as a test, and what result would show we are wrong. Nothing here is medical advice.

## Sensory processing disorders and connectivity

People who react strongly to perfumes and strong smells (chemical intolerance) are such that they perceive a smell at a constant level of intensity, the signal is processed at the same intensity over time. Normal people perceive the signal intensity to decrease over time, they "get used to it". Signal intensity is monitored via brain blood flow (measured using EEG and fMRI). The same blood flow pattern as those with chemical intolerance is found in those with chronic pain.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-1">1</a><span>,</span><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup>

## GABA, the Brake on Glutamate

If glutamate is the accelerator, GABA is the brake. It is the brain's main inhibitory neurotransmitter, and the body makes it out of glutamate: an enzyme called glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) does the conversion. That enzyme is also one of the main things the immune system attacks in type 1 diabetes. Antibodies against GAD (anti-GAD65) are one of the standard autoantibodies used to diagnose and predict it.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup> So the enzyme that turns the accelerator into the brake is a target in the same autoimmune disease that comes up with high sensitivity and celiac.

Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) can measure GABA directly in a living brain. In 37 autistic children and 35 typically developing children, GABA was lower in the autistic children's sensorimotor cortex, the part that processes touch, but normal in the visual cortex. Lower GABA went with differences on touch tasks.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup> The picture is not settled: a study of autistic adults found no difference in GABA.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-6">6</a></sup> The idea behind both studies is an old one, that autism comes from too much excitation relative to inhibition in sensory and social systems.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-7">7</a></sup>

Gut bacteria make GABA too. Out of 91 strains taken from the human intestine, one Lactobacillus and four Bifidobacterium strains turned glutamate into GABA, the best of them up to 90% of it.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-8">8</a></sup> Bacteroides make large amounts of GABA, and in people with major depression, more Bacteroides in the stool went with weaker depression-linked signatures on brain scans.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-9">9</a></sup> These are the same groups of bacteria that are lower in autism and that drop under stress.

In mice, feeding a Lactobacillus strain changed GABA receptor levels in the brain and lowered anxiety- and depression-like behaviour, and none of this happened when the vagus nerve was cut.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-10">10</a></sup> The same strain did nothing measurable for stress or cognition in healthy men.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-11">11</a></sup>

_Remark: GABA made in the gut is not known to reach the brain directly, and the mouse results have not carried over to people so far. What this section shows is that the gut can make and respond to the brake, not that it sets the brain's balance._

## SPD causing Visual Tunneling and Autoimmune conditions affecting Eyesight

Visual tunneling is a particularly pervasive issue that affects all senses, it results from an overload of visual sensation in the brain. Tunneling is a subconscious spatial adaptation to reduce the information the individual handles. The individual is either overwhelmed with informatio or has trouble sorting out the significant area for attention.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-12">12</a></sup>

"Cognitively speaking, for these individuals to succeed, they subconsciously fragment space or tunnel, i.e. reduce the amount of information processed in order not to be overwhelmed in dealing with abstract or conceptual activities. In the physiological aspect, the complications of this adaptation for the hyperopic patient include moving the pelvis backward and the forehead forward, and the eyes feel like they are turned out."  This visual tunnelling affects their ability to control their attention, as well as motor and postural changes. For example, one might walk with smaller steps, round their shoulders, lock their knees when standing, and develop upper back, shoulder, or neck tension.

Nutrient deficiences such as low vitamin D as well as being sensorily overwhelmed cause eyesight issues (autoimmune diseases of the eye and visual tunneling respectively). For vitamin D what has been shown is an association: people with low vitamin D were about three times as likely to have noninfectious anterior uveitis.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-13">13</a></sup>

## Sensory processing disorders and Autoimmune Disease

People who are highly sensitive (a genetic trait) are more likely to have autoimmune diseases such as diabetes, and I would postulate, celiac.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-14">14</a></sup> Celiac and diabetes are often comorbid: about 6% of people with type 1 diabetes have biopsy-proven celiac disease.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-15">15</a></sup>

This study does not show causalty, it shows that 50% of children with autism also suffer gastrointestinal symptoms, such as celiac disease or constipation, comorbidly. Children with ASD have altered gut microbial composition, with decreased gut bacterial variety and underestimation of potentially helpful bacteria such as Bifidobacterium, Prevotella, and Desulfovibrio, as well as a decrease in short-chain fatty acids.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-16">16</a></sup>

It runs the other way too: in 3.56 million Danes, a prior autoimmune disease raised the risk of a later mood disorder by 45%.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-17">17</a></sup>

## The Enteric Nervous System is a Sensory System

We tend to talk about the gut as something our senses act on. It is more accurate to say the gut is itself a sense organ, and it is built out of the same parts as touch and taste.

Your gut has its own sensory neurons (intrinsic primary afferent neurons, or IPANs), and they can run a reflex from start to finish without asking the brain. This was only firmly shown in the mid-1990s. Something chemical or mechanical sets them off, they tell the interneurons, the interneurons tell the motor neurons.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-18">18</a></sup> There is also work suggesting these neurons are not specialists, each one does several jobs and they act together as a sensory network, and this holds across different parts of the gut and across species.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-19">19</a></sup>

Now look at the lining of the gut. Enterochromaffin cells carry chemosensory receptors, are electrically excitable, and talk through synaptic contacts to serotonin-sensitive nerve fibres<sup class="src"><a href="#src-20">20</a></sup>. They are also literally touch sensors: press on them and they release serotonin, and they express Piezo2, which is the same channel you use for light touch and for knowing where your limbs are<sup class="src"><a href="#src-21">21</a></sup>. Beyder's group compares them to the Merkel cells in your skin<sup class="src"><a href="#src-21">21</a></sup>.

Then there are neuropod cells, which synapse directly onto the vagus nerve and pass along what is inside the gut within milliseconds, using glutamate<sup class="src"><a href="#src-22">22</a></sup>. So the inside of your gut is one synapse away from your brainstem.

The same genes show up in both places. Over 90% of the 62 top-ranked autism risk genes (in SFARI) are expressed in the gut as well as in the brain<sup class="src"><a href="#src-23">23</a></sup>. Cntnap2, a synaptic adhesion molecule which matters for sensory processing, is expressed mainly in enteric sensory neurons<sup class="src"><a href="#src-24">24</a></sup>. SHANK3 is present in the myenteric plexus, and mice missing it have guts that are built differently and work differently<sup class="src"><a href="#src-25">25</a></sup>.

Lauren Orefice's lab reports that in mouse models of autism, the nerves which run from the gut to the spinal cord (dorsal root ganglion neurons) send heightened signals<sup class="src"><a href="#src-26">26</a></sup>. The same lab found that mutations in the peripheral neurons alone were enough to make a mouse over-react to touch.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-27">27</a></sup>

What about in humans? In 2,973 autistic children, every type of chronic GI problem came with higher rates of sensory over-responsivity and of anxiety, and each of those independently predicted GI problems<sup class="src"><a href="#src-28">28</a></sup>. A smaller study found the same combined sensory and GI profile in ADHD as well as in autism<sup class="src"><a href="#src-29">29</a></sup>. These studies do not show causality, they are correlations from parent reports.

The microbiome sets how excitable these neurons are. Mice raised germ-free have less excitable enteric sensory neurons, and they need a microbiome for their nerves to work normally at all<sup class="src"><a href="#src-30">30</a><span>,</span><a href="#src-31">31</a></sup>. It goes the other way too, serine proteases made by commensal bacteria calm down dorsal root ganglion neurons<sup class="src"><a href="#src-32">32</a></sup>.

And the gut can get stuck. If enterochromaffin cells are kept activated for long enough you get lasting visceral hypersensitivity, even with no inflammation to start it<sup class="src"><a href="#src-33">33</a></sup>. I would postulate that this is the gut's version of never getting used to the perfume.

_Remark: Whether glutamate does anything inside the enteric nervous system itself is contested. One review says all of the glutamatergic machinery is there<sup class="src"><a href="#src-34">34</a></sup>, but Wood's lab found that neither glutamate nor its receptor agonists excited enteric neurons<sup class="src"><a href="#src-35">35</a></sup>. The result we trust is the synapse from the neuropod cells to the vagus._

_Remark: Nearly all of the mechanism above is from mice. We have not found a study which tests sensory processing disorder on its own against the enteric nervous system._

## Hypersensitivity that Outlasts the Stimulus

The study that matters most for this essay is Bayrer et al. (2023).<sup class="src"><a href="#src-33">33</a></sup> They switched enterochromaffin cells up and down in living mice. Turning them up was enough to make the gut hypersensitive to stretching. The cells were also needed for a bacterial short-chain fatty acid, isovalerate, to sensitize the gut. And when the cells were kept active for long enough, the hypersensitivity stayed after the activation stopped, with no inflammation ever starting it.

That last result is the one I care about. The first section of this essay is about people who never get used to a smell: the signal stays at full intensity when it should fade. Bayrer's mice show the gut version of the same thing, a sensitivity that persists after its cause is gone. If one state links these conditions, persistence of this kind is a good candidate for what that state is, and it can be measured over time instead of asked about once.

Two more findings from the same paper point the same way. Changing enterochromaffin activity also produced anxiety-like behaviour, which went back to normal when serotonin signalling was blocked. And the circuit behaved differently by sex: in females it appeared to be engaged all the time, which fits irritable bowel syndrome affecting about three times as many women as men.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-33">33</a></sup>

_Remark: this is in mice, with genetic tools no one would use in a person. It shows that persistence of this kind exists and has a circuit. It does not show that it happens in people with sensory processing disorder._

## Enteric Nervous System and Excitable Mental Illness

Our guts produce 90% of serotonin, if you are low on folic acid or any other basic vitamins/nutrients you literally cannot create serotonin, dopamine, and other neurotransmitters<sup class="src"><a href="#src-36">36</a></sup>. Would a severe serotonin deficiency show up in a SPECT scan? SPECT cannot see serotonin itself, but it can see the serotonin transporter, and in a small study people with major depression had less transporter binding in the midbrain, the more depressed the less<sup class="src"><a href="#src-37">37</a></sup>.

It is the bacteria that set the level: spore-forming gut bacteria push the enterochromaffin cells to make serotonin (measured in mice, and with human microbiota).<sup class="src"><a href="#src-38">38</a></sup>

There is ongoing work to directly detect and monitor this for diagnostic and treatment purposes.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-39">39</a></sup>

The overall changing of gut bacterial community in terms of β-diversity was consistently observed in ASD patients compared with HCs. Furthermore, Bifidobacterium, Blautia, Dialister, Prevotella, Veillonella, and Turicibacter were consistently decreased, while Lactobacillus, Bacteroides, Desulfovibrio, and Clostridium were increased in patients with ASD relative to HCs in certain studies.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-40">40</a><span>,</span><a href="#src-41">41</a></sup>

Stress changes who lives in your gut. A single two-hour social stressor is enough to shift the bacterial community in mice, and Lactobacillus drops under stress in rodents, primates and humans.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-42">42</a></sup>

There has been some recent research on fecal transplants curing some forms of mental illness. The inverse is also true "with the transmission of depressive and anxiety-like symptoms and behaviors resulting from the transplantation of microbiota from psychiatrically ill donors to healthy recipients". ## Enteric Nervous system/SPD causing Lowered Immune System causing Autoimmune Disease

If you have a lower immune system (low igA or igG or igM) you are more likely to have an autoimmune disease.

_Remark: As a note, you are also more likely than average to have an autoimmune disease with an abnormally strong immune system. This is not the case we are discussing here._

The enteric nervous system generates igA and igG in our body. There is a link between igA and levels with a person’s perception of stress, implying the nervous system has the ability to control igA levels.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-48">48</a></sup>

People with IgA deficiency have a different gut microbiota from everyone else, even though their bodies secrete IgM to compensate.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-49">49</a></sup>

People with IgA deficiency should be tested for celiac disease because they are 10 to 20 times likely to develop an autoimmune response to gluten than the general population.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-50">50</a></sup>

Using tests of raised igA and igG to detect for celiac disease is harmful and ineffective, as having naturally lowered igA and igG is usually comorbid with celiac disease. Thus, the rasied igA and igG register as normal levels on tests, giving many patients false negatives. The most effective test is a DNA test for the celiac markers.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-51">51</a></sup>

Immunosenescence or the aging of the immune system is linked to the increase of autoantibody frequency while the antibodies decline<sup class="src"><a href="#src-52">52</a></sup>.  One way to frame the connection between SPD and being immunocompromised is that higher sensitivity leads to greater stress / perceived stress, which leads to a decrease in immunity which then naturally causes an increase in autoantibodies that would otherwise have occured during someones natural immunosenescence.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-53">53</a></sup>

In 106,464 Swedes with a stress-related disorder, the risk of a later autoimmune disease was 36% higher, and 46% higher with PTSD.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-54">54</a></sup>

"As in the salivary gland, sIgA is rapidly secreted in mucosal explants from porcine distal colon in response to both cholinergic and adrenergic receptor agonists, but remains unaltered by acute exposure to an enteroadherent microbial pathogen. ## How to Tell One Cause from Several

### The evidence is pairwise

Every link in this essay connects two things at a time, and the pairs come from different populations. The autoimmune and psychiatric links come from national registries of diagnostic codes in adults: autoimmune disease before mood disorders in 3.56 million Danes,<sup class="src"><a href="#src-17">17</a></sup> and stress-related disorders before autoimmune disease in 106,464 Swedes.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-54">54</a></sup> These registries say nothing about sensory processing. The sensory, gut and anxiety links come from autistic children, rated by their parents: 2,973 children in the Autism Treatment Network,<sup class="src"><a href="#src-28">28</a></sup> and a smaller cross-disorder study.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-29">29</a></sup> Those cohorts say nothing about autoimmune disease.

A chain of pairs cannot answer the question. If sensitivity goes with gut problems in one group, and autoimmunity goes with mood disorders in another, both one shared cause and several overlapping causes would produce that. You need all of the conditions measured in the same people.

### Predictions

The two answers predict different data.

- **One shared state.** Once you know a person's level of that state, knowing their gut symptoms tells you nothing more about their sensory reactivity, anxiety or autoimmune risk. The correlations across domains run entirely through the shared state, and disappear once you condition on it.
- **Several states.** Correlations are left over after conditioning on the best single state, and they cluster: for example sensory with anxiety, gut with autoimmune. A model with two or more factors fits clearly better than one.

What would refute the one-state version:

- Leftover cross-domain correlations as large as the raw ones, after conditioning on the best single state.
- A two-factor model that fits clearly better and splits the sensory and immune sides apart.
- A shared state that does not predict persistence, the failure to habituate from the first section, measured directly.

### Methods sketch

The model is a latent-variable model: each person has one latent state (or several, in the competing model), and every observation is a noisy readout of it. The observations need their own measurement models, because they are noisy in different ways:

- **Parent reports** of sensory and gut symptoms are ordinal, and they depend on the parent as well as the child.
- **Diagnostic codes** are binary. They miss cases, and whether a code is recorded depends on how often someone sees a doctor.

The data to fit it on exist. SPARK, the Simons Foundation's cohort, was built to recruit 50,000 autism families with genetic data.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-63">63</a></sup> The Simons Simplex Collection has about 2,600 families with one autistic child, and their unaffected siblings, with deep phenotyping.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-64">64</a></sup> Both collect parent-reported medical history next to genetics. Which sensory, gut and immune items each one actually has is the first thing to check.

The hard part is inference. The quantity we want is how strongly the domains move together. The usual fast method for models this size, factorized ("mean-field") variational inference, sets the posterior correlations between latent quantities to zero and underestimates their variances.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-65">65</a></sup> For this question that is the worst possible failure: it is biased toward saying the domains don't move together, and it is overconfident while it does so. The fit needs full-rank or structured approximations, checked against exact sampling on subsets.

### Genetics

If the shared state is real, it should have genetic roots. Over 90% of the 62 top-ranked autism risk genes are expressed in the gut as well as the brain.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-23">23</a></sup> Common variants matter too: the first large genome-wide study of autism found five places in the genome where common variants raise the risk.<sup class="src"><a href="#src-66">66</a></sup> The test is whether the latent state tracks rare damaging variants in these genes, or polygenic risk for autism. If there is one state, a gene's effects on the senses, the gut and mood should all pass through it. A gene that changes gut symptoms without changing the state is evidence for more than one. The siblings in the Simons Simplex Collection let you compare within families.

### An animal readout

Bayrer's result suggests the readout: switch on enterochromaffin cells, then watch how long the effect lasts. A single behavioural test at one time point cannot see that. Recording a mouse's ultrasonic vocalizations in its home cage can, continuously and without handling the animal. I built software for exactly this kind of monitoring at Vium ([Learning the ultrasonic language of rodents](/mouse-vocalizations/)). The prediction is that calls change after activation and stay changed after it stops, on the same time course as the visceral hypersensitivity. How mouse calls relate to pain and anxiety is itself only partly understood, so this would need checking against the standard tests first.
